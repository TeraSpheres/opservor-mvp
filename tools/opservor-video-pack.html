Build the twenty-second one first

The full film is eighty-eight seconds and takes an afternoon. The short cut takes about forty minutes, uses six of the same shots, and is the version that actually travels — on LinkedIn, TikTok, Instagram and YouTube alike, the short vertical clip is the one the feed pushes. Nothing here is wasted either way: build the six, cut the short, and if it lands you already own a third of the long film.

Different job, same story. The long film is for someone who has already stopped. The short one is for someone who has not. So it drops the conversation entirely and runs on captions — most people watch with the sound off, and a twenty-second clip has no time to wait for a voice to finish a sentence.

How this gets made

The hard part of AI video is not the pictures. It is keeping the same five faces across sixteen shots. This pack is built so that problem is solved once, at the start, and never again.

  1. Make the cast first. Generate one clean portrait for each of the five people below, using the portrait prompt on their card. Keep the best one. Save it. These five images are now your cast — do not regenerate them.
  2. Feed each face back in. Every shot prompt says which characters are in it. Attach that person's saved portrait as a reference image (in most tools this is character reference, image prompt or consistent character). That is what stops Marcus turning into a different man halfway through.
  3. Turn each still into five seconds. Take the still into an image-to-video tool and use the motion line from the same shot card. Short, simple movement. Five seconds per shot, sixteen shots.
  4. Assemble and voice it in CapCut. Drop the clips in order, add the dialogue as captions, add the voices. See the voice section — there is a fix for the problem you hit last time.
One decision that saves the whole project: do not try to make the characters' mouths match the words. Lip sync is where these projects die. Shoot the talking wide, over-the-shoulder, or with the speaker turned away, and carry the dialogue in voice-over plus on-screen captions. This is how most corporate film is cut anyway, it looks more expensive, and it removes the one step that never comes out right. Every shot below is already framed for it.

Put one small grey line at the bottom of the end card: Dramatisation. Product screens show demo data. Nobody minds a dramatised scene — every enterprise vendor makes them — but the line is what keeps a fictional worker praising your product from ever being read as a real customer quote. It costs you a second and it protects the claim.

The voices

Five speaking lines from four people, none longer than a hundred characters. That is the whole voice job.

The 500-character wall you hit last time was per text block, not per project. Generate each line as its own text element and the limit never comes near you — the longest line in this film is 82 characters. Do not paste the script in as one block.
CharacterVoice to pickLines
DanaOlder woman, low, unhurried, slightly flat. Authority without volume.3
MarcusMan, warm mid-range, a little worn down. Not corporate.4
PriyaWoman, crisp, measured, no rush at all.2
JoeOlder man, gravelly, plain-spoken. The most human voice in it.2

CapCut's own text-to-speech will do this if the four voices sound different enough from each other. If they do not, ElevenLabs' free tier gives you far more separation and is worth the twenty minutes. Under the dialogue, run one quiet ambient warehouse bed — distant forklift hum, faint reversing beeper — at about 15% volume. Silence under AI voices sounds fake; room tone fixes it instantly.

Nobody was wrong. That is exactly what makes it so hard to catch.
