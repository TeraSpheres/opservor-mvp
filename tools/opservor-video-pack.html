Why the story changed

The original script was built on a capacity clash — vehicles in for service on a busy dock day. That check cannot run on this data: it works out which vehicles serve which site by matching a trip's starting point against a site's name, exactly, and none of them match. So there was no such finding to show.

What Guardian did produce is better. Ten items from one supplier. Nine of them already past the point where ordering could save them. The most urgent has zero days of cover and goes out at 7.62 a day. One order fixes all ten.

It is a sharper argument than the one it replaced. A dock-and-maintenance clash needs explaining. "Nine items were already too late and the screen never said so" does not — and it lands on anyone who has ever run stock, which is a far larger audience than people who run docks and fleets together.

In CapCut

  1. New project, set the ratio to 1:1. Square. Your images are landscape, so square crops off far less than vertical would, and LinkedIn treats square as well as it treats vertical. That is where you are posting.
  2. Import all five files and drag them onto the timeline in the order above.
  3. Set each clip's duration. Four seconds each, except the finding at five and the end card at three. Click the clip, then drag its edge or type the length.
  4. Add the movement. Select a clip → Animation → Zoom in or Pan. Keep every move slow. If it is noticeable, it is too fast.
  5. Add the captions. One text block per shot, centred, large, held for the whole clip. No word-by-word animation — it makes short clips unreadable.
  6. Add the narration. See the voice section below — one voice, four lines, generated from the caption blocks you just made.
  7. Add music, no lyrics. Something low that builds. Search CapCut's library for "corporate documentary" or "cinematic tension". Drop it to about 20% so it sits under the voice rather than against it.
  8. Export at 1080×1080, 30fps and upload straight to LinkedIn as a video post.
On the captions: put them in the lower third, not the middle. The middle of shot 4 is your product screen, and a caption across it wastes the only frame that shows what you built.

The voice

One narrator, not five. This is narration, not a conversation — a single steady voice across twenty seconds reads as authored, where several voices read as a slideshow with actors.

The 500-character limit that stopped you last time is per text block, not per project. Each line below is under sixty characters, so you will not come near it. Do not paste the script in as one block — generate each caption separately, which is what you are doing anyway since each is already its own text element.

Every line fits inside its shot with a beat to spare, so nothing needs re-timing. Leave shot 5 silent. A voice reading out your own company name at the end sounds like an advert; music under a still end card sounds like the end of a film.

Doing it in CapCut

  1. Click a text block on the timeline. The one already holding that shot's caption.
  2. Find "Text to speech". In the text panel on the right, under the font and style options.
  3. Pick a calm narrator voice. Avoid anything labelled energetic, upbeat or advertising. You want the voice of someone stating a fact, not selling. Try the deeper documentary-style options first.
  4. Generate, then repeat for the other three. Same voice every time. Changing voice between lines is the single fastest way to make it sound cheap.
  5. Drop the music to about 20%. Once there are voices, music at its default volume will fight them. The voice leads; the music fills the gaps.
If the voice rushes the line, add a full stop where you want it to breathe — "Nine of them were already past saving. Opservor said so." breathes at that full stop. Text-to-speech paces off punctuation, so punctuation is your timing control.

If CapCut's voices sound too robotic for you, ElevenLabs' free tier gives noticeably better narration for four short lines and takes about twenty minutes to set up. Worth it only if the CapCut result bothers you — for a twenty-second clip under music, it usually will not.

The post

Three lines. The video carries the argument; the caption only has to earn the comment.

Ten items. One supplier. Nine of them already past the point where ordering could save them.

Every one had stock on the shelf. Every one was above zero. Nothing anywhere said a word.

A stock screen tells you what you have. It does not tell you how long it lasts.

If you run stock — has any system you have used ever divided one by the other?
